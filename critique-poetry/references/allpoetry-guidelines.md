# AllPoetry critique & craft guidelines

Distilled from allpoetry.com/guides (Commenting, Improve, Tips, Show Don't Tell, Cliche, Rhyme & Forced Rhyme), fetched 2026-08-07.

## Know your audience (from "Commenting for Growth")

Two primary poet types; identify which you're addressing before critiquing:

- **The Student** — goal: growth. Wants honest, critical feedback; looking for cliches to kill and metaphors to sharpen. "Don't hold back, I want to learn!"
- **The Empath** — goal: connection. Often a sensitive new poet or someone writing for healing. Values empathy and shared experience over technical critique. "Thank you for feeling this with me."

If the author's note says "be nice" or mentions the poem is personal, stick to empathy and encouragement.

## Three parts of a great critique

1. **What works** — always start with something positive; name a specific line or story element that resonated.
2. **Constructive critique** — identify specific areas for improvement (a line that felt forced, a confusing metaphor, faltering rhythm). Frame as suggestion: "I wonder if…", "For me, the second stanza…"
3. **Personal response** — what feelings the poem evoked; personal experience with the subject.

Being kind but honest: sandwich method (start and end positive), focus on 1–2 major points rather than overwhelming, and soften tone ("In my opinion") because tone is hard to read online.

## Show, don't tell (the most important craft lens)

Telling states an abstract emotion; showing uses sensory detail so the reader feels it. Flag abstract words like *sad, happy, angry, beautiful* and passive emotion statements ("I am so sad"). Strong poems anchor in sight, sound, smell, touch, taste — ideally two senses in one line. Every poem should hide a "secret truth" revealed through images rather than stated; telling readers what to feel robs them of discovering it.

Examples:
- "She loved him with all her soul" → "The moment of silence, eyes closed, tracing the pulse on his wrist."
- "He was incredibly lonely" → "He set the table for two, then slowly moved the second plate back into the cupboard."

## Cliches

Cliches are "stolen" feelings — shorthand the reader's brain skips over (heart of gold, tears like rain, love is a rose, silent as the grave). Push toward the poet's own specific sensory experience: not "blue as the ocean" but "the blue of a gas flame." Suggest the substitution exercise: circle every familiar phrase, spend five minutes re-describing it in strictly sensory terms.

## Rhyme & forced rhyme

Forced rhyme = a word chosen strictly for sound, producing filler lines, nonsense imagery, melodrama, or inverted "Yoda-speak" grammar. It's the #1 killer of amateur poems: sound gets chosen over meaning. Remedies to suggest:
- Read aloud — stumbling or embarrassment marks a forced line.
- Change the *first* word of a rhyme pair, not the second.
- Use slant/near rhymes (bridge/grudge) or internal rhyme instead of rigid end-rhyme.
- Master free verse first; let the music emerge from imagery and rhythm.
- Don't break grammar for a rhyme.

## Common pitfalls checklist (from "Problems & Solutions")

1. **Abstract emotion trap** — telling feelings instead of showing them through a scene.
2. **Capitalization & punctuation** — capitalizing every line or random Title Caps; punctuation should work like a musical score.
3. **Line & stanza rhythm** — long wrapping lines, no breathing room; aim for 4–7 line stanzas, breaks that create power.
4. **Adjective overdose** — judgment words (*beautiful, horrible, vast, lovely*) tell readers what to think; prefer strong nouns and active verbs.
5. **Inverted syntax** — unnatural word order forced by rhyme; write as people speak.
6. **Vague vs. specific** — generic nouns (bird, car, flower) have no texture; the exact thing (crow, Ford) creates authority.

Make every word count — cut anything vague, obvious, redundant, or that doesn't advance the scene. Poetry is distilled emotion: if it doesn't need to be there, cut it.

## Verbatim comment requirements (shown on AllPoetry's comment form)

> If AI is used for comments, it needs to include a LOT of human additions - just AI is not allowed here. Most people can tell and want human feedback.
> Each comment MUST include AT LEAST 2 suggestions and 1 encouragement. Other great things:
> - Analysis of title, first-line, last-line
> - Pacing, stanza breaks, line length
> - Word-flow, rhyming, form, variety, emotional content, concrete descriptions
> - Awkward/brilliant areas, drama, randomness, poetic-feeling, and your reaction.
> - A step-by-step walk-through of your reading experience/understanding

## Broader craft principles (from "10 Steps")

- Editing provides the shape; question every word across multiple sittings.
- Read work out loud — the ear catches awkward rhythm the eye misses.
- Rhyme is a trap for beginners; imagery and rhythm first.
